# American Pragmatism and Incarnational Economics

Tags: `philosophy` `economics` `pragmatism` · 🔭 fieldwork

There's something odd going on between American pragmatism and incarnational theology that most people walk right past. Both reject the idea that truth is something you watch from the stands. Both say reality is participatory. You're in it, not above it.

For William James, truth wasn't correspondence to some pre-existing reality but "what works in the way of belief." For incarnational theology, God doesn't observe creation from outside but enters it, transforms it from within. The overlap is hard to ignore: truth happens through concrete engagement, not abstract correspondence.

This matters for economics.

Traditional economic theory inherits the spectator stance. We model markets, optimize within constraints, calculate equilibria. We act as if economic reality sits out there, independent of us, waiting to be discovered. The economist as detached observer, finding truth through better models.

But my fieldwork with startups keeps showing me something else. Every pivot, every feature decision, every architectural choice doesn't discover economic truth. It creates it. When a platform changes its algorithm, it doesn't reveal user preferences. It reshapes what preferences can even exist.

The American pragmatists understood this. Dewey's experimentalism wasn't about testing hypotheses against fixed reality. It was about creating new realities through intelligent action. Truth emerges through practice, not prior to it.

This is incarnational in the deeper sense, not as specific Christian doctrine but as a pattern: reality transforms through participation, not description. The divine doesn't dictate from outside but works through material engagement.

Consider how this reframes economic thinking. The traditional view says: markets exist, we model them, we optimize within them. The incarnational view reverses the arrow: we act economically, our actions create constraints, and new realities emerge through practice.

This isn't relativism. Some practices work, others don't. Some create flourishing, others create capture. But the difference from the textbook picture is that we're not discovering pre-existing laws. We're participating in their creation.

The startup world gets this intuitively. "Build, measure, learn" isn't just methodology. It's pragmatist metaphysics wearing a hoodie. You don't discover product-market fit like finding buried treasure. You create it through iterative engagement. The market you end up fitting didn't exist before you started building.

And yet we still talk as if we're discovering rather than creating. Still model as if observing rather than participating. Still optimize as if constraints were given rather than made.

What would economic theory look like if it took incarnation seriously? If it recognized that every model, metric, and market structure doesn't just describe reality but enters into it?

Metrics don't measure value, they constitute it. Markets don't reveal preferences, they construct them. Models don't describe equilibria, they create them. The observer is always inside the system being observed. You can't subtract yourself from the equation.

The pragmatists knew this. Christians live and preach a personalized understanding of this philosophical intuition. Economics is still catching up.
